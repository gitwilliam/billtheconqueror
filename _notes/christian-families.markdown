It is sometimes argued that because we may speak of families as Christian, we
may also speak of nations as Christian (or other non-ecclesiastical
organizations). This polemical argument loses its footing when we consider a
fundamental difference between families and nations as viewed by the Bible and
the Westminster Standards. That is, members of families receive the sign and
seal of the Covenant of Grace; members or citizens of nations (or other
non-ecclesiastical organizations) do not.

A family may properly be considered Christian, with at least one believing
parent, because the children of the family are entitled to receive the sign of
the Covenant of Grace by virtue of their being born into the family
(1 Cor. 7:14; WCF 28.4; WLC 166). The Confession also treats the family as an
organization that should worship together daily (WCF 21.6).

Nowhere in Scripture or our Confession are citizens of modern nations to receive
the sign and seal of the Covenant of Grace by virtue of their being born
citizens of that nation. They may receive non-redemptive "signs and seals" in
that case (birth certificates, Social Security numbers, etc.), but those are in
no way Christian signs and seals of the Covenant of Grace.

Of course Old Testament Israel was a nation whose members received the covenant
sign by birth, but its role as the covenant nation is fulfilled in the church,
not in any modern state (WCF 25.2). In a sense, we may speak of the New Covenant
community as a Christian nation, one that knows no physical land boundaries,
unlike modern nations. Paul calls the household of a believer holy
(1 Cor. 7:14), and Peter calls the church a holy nation (1 Pet. 2:9). Neither
the Bible nor the Confession applies this language to any other nation (or
organization) under the New Covenant.

This is not an argument for or against so-called Christian nations, but rather
an argument against using the Christian family analogy to conclude that nations
may also be Christian.
